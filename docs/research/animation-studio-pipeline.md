# How professional studios produce animated features and series

Research note, 1 Nov 2026. Industry practice only. Sources are linked inline; where a claim rests on a secondary or practitioner source, or where studios differ, it is marked **(uncertain)** or **(varies)**.

## Summary

- Studios **edit before they animate**. The film first exists as a story reel (boards plus scratch voices, temp music and sound), cut by the editorial department, which is involved from the start of development ([Slate](https://www.slate.com/articles/video/video/2017/02/how_editing_works_for_animated_films_video.html); [ProVideo Coalition, Spider-Verse](https://www.provideocoalition.com/aotc-spiderverse)).
- The reel is rebuilt many times and screened to the studio and to trusted peers at intervals (every four months on *Soul*) until it works. Only then do sequences go into expensive production ([ProVideo Coalition, Soul](https://www.provideocoalition.com/art-of-the-cut-soul/amp/); [Fast Company, Braintrust](https://www.fastcompany.com/3027135/inside-the-pixar-braintrust)).
- Disney Animation groups production into **Development → Asset Creation → Shot Production → Post Production** ([Disney Animation, Process](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/)). Pixar's public pipeline has nine stages, from story and art to rendering ([Science Behind Pixar](https://sciencebehindpixar.org/explore)).
- The **sequence** is the creative unit: one location and story point, one look, one lighting setup. The **shot** is the unit that is tracked, assigned and approved. Each shot moves through layout, animation, FX, lighting and compositing, and its latest version replaces the earlier one in the editorial cut.
- Look is set **top-down**. A colour script covers the whole film, then sequence keys, then a few lit key shots per sequence. Other lighters propagate those key setups to the remaining shots.
- Animation is approved **in stages**. The director signs off blocking before the animator refines and polishes, so that expensive work is never spent on a rejected idea.
- Reviews are frequent and graded: daily dailies, director reviews (historically the "sweatbox") and periodic full-film screenings. Notes and versions live in a production-tracking system such as Autodesk Flow Production Tracking (formerly ShotGrid).
- 2D/TV pipelines replace layout and CG departments with an animatic, timing on exposure sheets (X-sheets), animation at an outsourced service studio, and a retake loop after delivery.

## 1. Pipeline stages in order

The order below is the common feature order. Disney Animation groups the stages into four phases ([Disney Animation, Process](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/)). Pixar's public exhibit lists Story & Art, Modeling, Rigging, Surfaces, Sets & Cameras, Animation, Simulation, Lighting and Rendering ([Science Behind Pixar](https://sciencebehindpixar.org/explore)). Stages overlap heavily in practice: different sequences sit in different stages at the same time.

| Stage | What it produces |
| --- | --- |
| **Development** | Premise, story treatment, early visual development and casting. Disney places Story, Visual Development, Editorial & Sound and Casting all in Development ([Disney](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/)). Pixar develops a film for about two to three years before production ([Soul editor Kevin Nolting](https://www.provideocoalition.com/art-of-the-cut-soul/amp/)). |
| **Script** | A screenplay that keeps changing through production. On *Into the Spider-Verse*, sequences could start as boards from an idea before a full script existed, and the script evolved to the end ([Robert Fisher Jr.](https://www.provideocoalition.com/aotc-spiderverse)). |
| **Visual development / look development** | Character, set and colour design, then (in CG) surfacing and shading of the built assets. Disney lists "Look Development" under Asset Creation ([Disney](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/)). The **colour script** is a sequence of small paintings that map colour and emotion across the whole film (see §2). |
| **Story / storyboards** | Thousands of panels, pitched to the director and redrawn many times. *Soul* used 73,811 boards ([ProVideo Coalition](https://www.provideocoalition.com/art-of-the-cut-soul/amp/)). One account says each scene is boarded several ways and a film can use up to 80,000 boards ([Bordwell](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2008/04/10/a-glimpse-into-the-pixar-kitchen/)). |
| **Story reel / animatic** (historically "Leica reel") | The boards timed to scratch dialogue, temp music and sound effects, played as a film ([Wikipedia: Leica reel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leica_reel)). **Editorial owns it from day one.** Animation editors join "from the very, very beginning" and help develop the story ([Fisher](https://www.provideocoalition.com/aotc-spiderverse)). Nolting calls story artists and editors co-writers ([Soul](https://www.provideocoalition.com/art-of-the-cut-soul/amp/)). The first *Spider-Verse* cut was assembled in about ten weeks, while the boards were still early ([Fisher](https://www.provideocoalition.com/aotc-spiderverse)). |
| **Voice recording** | *Scratch* voices come first: studio staff record temporary lines, often before the actors are cast ([Wikipedia: Scratch vocal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_vocal)). On *Spider-Verse*, office staff recorded scratch and editors cut the best takes into a "radio play" of each scene ([Fisher](https://www.provideocoalition.com/aotc-spiderverse)). *Final* cast sessions replace the scratch lines as the story stabilises. Animation is then performed to the recorded dialogue ([Soul](https://www.provideocoalition.com/art-of-the-cut-soul/amp/)). Actors usually record alone, not together ([Bordwell](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2008/04/10/a-glimpse-into-the-pixar-kitchen/)). A scratch voice is occasionally kept, for example Joe Ranft as Heimlich ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scratch_vocal)). |
| **Layout / previs** | Turns boards into 3D shots, setting the camera, lens, staging and the cut points between shots. On *Spider-Verse*, approved boards were split into shots and sent to camera layout, which returned rough blocked figures after several iterations ([Fisher](https://www.provideocoalition.com/aotc-spiderverse)). For action, layout may shoot master and closer coverage that editors cut "live-action style" ([Bordwell](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2008/04/10/a-glimpse-into-the-pixar-kitchen/)). Large studios often split layout into *rough layout* (camera and blocking for editorial) and *final layout* (final assets, camera adjusted after animation) ([fwdlabs](https://fwdlabs.com/blog/?p=3420), **uncertain**: practitioner blog). |
| **Modelling, rigging, surfacing** | The built characters, sets and props. Rigs are the controls animators use; Mike Wazowski is reported to have about 7,000 controls ([Science Behind Pixar coverage](https://amt-lab.org/blog/2021/10/n1i8aobru0vxw0yvcreb9vzlldl6z9), **uncertain**). Disney places these stages, plus simulation setup, under Asset Creation ([Disney](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/)). |
| **Animation** | Character performance per shot. The director briefs the animator, the animator blocks the shot, the director approves the blocking, and only then is the shot refined and polished ([Disney, Frozen 2](https://www.disney.co.uk/movies/frozen-2/behind-the-scenes-of-frozen-2); [Blender Studio guide](https://studio.blender.org/tools/artist-guide/shot-production/animation)). **Stepped vs splined:** in stepped blocking, keys hold with no in-betweens, so the review is about poses and timing; in spline, the software interpolates. Many animators stay stepped, adding breakdowns, then spline late. The choice is per artist and per studio **(varies)** ([Pluralsight](https://www.pluralsight.com/resources/blog/software-development/stepped-vs-spline-curves-blocking-animation)). One Disney animator's account says a scene typically reaches the director about three times ([Wikipedia: Sweat box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweat_box), **uncertain**). |
| **Technical animation, FX, crowds** | Cloth, hair, simulation, effects animation (water, fire, magic) and crowds. Disney lists Technical Animation, Effects Animation, Crowds and Set Extension inside Shot Production ([Disney](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/)). Some studios also build reusable FX "objects" so they do not have to hand-author every shot; Animal Logic re-runs shot-level FX rigs when upstream departments re-deliver ([Animal Logic, LEGO Batman](https://animallogic.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Lego-Batman-FX-Objects-Variations-and-Automation-Alternatives-to-Shot-Based-FX.pdf)). |
| **Lighting** | Light and colour per shot, built from the art department's keys (§2). |
| **Rendering** | Turns the lit 3D scene into images. Low-resolution renders serve work in progress; high-resolution renders are for the final film ([la Caixa, Pixar exhibit](https://mediahub.fundacionlacaixa.org/en/culture/animation/2024-05-07/pixar-s-animated-films-eight-key-steps-5890.html)). |
| **Compositing** | Assembles render layers into the final frame; in many studios lighters also composite their own shots (a DreamWorks lead lighter "sets up, lights, and composites key shots", per a [job listing](https://www.nbcunicareers.com/node/96643)) **(varies)**. |
| **Final edit, sound, grade** | Disney's Post Production phase covers final picture and sound delivery ([Disney](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/)). Temp music is used for most of production (*Soul* started on the *Arrival* score), and the composers join partway and feed stems into the cut ([Soul](https://www.provideocoalition.com/art-of-the-cut-soul/amp/)). On *Spider-Verse*, the Avid cut was conformed at a finishing house for the colour grade ([Fisher](https://www.provideocoalition.com/aotc-spiderverse)). |

## 2. Units of work

**Sequence vs shot.** A sequence is a group of shots that share a location and story point; a lighting lead sets up its lighting ([Disney lighting account](https://andersonuniversity.edu/?p=623), **uncertain**: secondary). A feature typically has 1,500 or more shots ([Bordwell](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2008/04/10/a-glimpse-into-the-pixar-kitchen/)). Story, colour and lighting decisions are made per sequence; assignments, versions and approvals are tracked per shot.

**How shots move.** Editorial breaks an approved story-reel sequence into shots and turns them over to layout. Each department then publishes new versions, and the editor drops the newest one into the timeline over the old one: boards → layout → rough animation → final animation → lighting. On *Spider-Verse*, editors kept only the newest layer in the timeline but could step back to earlier stages ([Fisher](https://www.provideocoalition.com/aotc-spiderverse)). "Shot turnover" means assigning a shot to an artist after layout ([AWN, Inspired 3D](https://www.awn.com/print/vfxworld/inspired-3d-getting-started-animated-short)).

**Shot packages.** A turnover carries the shot's frame range and cut length, camera, assets, dialogue audio and reference **(uncertain: no first-party definition of the term found)**. Blender Studio's published guide shows the practice: the animator checks the shot file, assets, frame rate and camera, gets a director briefing, and delivers a shot "ready to commit for lighting" ([Blender Studio](https://studio.blender.org/tools/artist-guide/shot-production/animation)).

**Colour scripts and lighting keys.** Ralph Eggleston introduced colour scripts at Pixar on *Toy Story*. They are small pastel paintings of every scene, a shorthand of the film's emotion in colour and light, and Pixar made one for every film afterwards ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/rip/ralph-eggleston-a-cornerstone-of-pixars-visual-style-dies-at-56-220781.html)). He then expanded each sequence into "sequence pastels" for the lighting department ([MoMA interview](https://www.moma.org/interactives/redstudio/interviews/behind/content/eggleston/assets/transcripts.php)). On *Toy Story 3*, the art director painted two or three colour keys per sequence ([Computer Graphics World](https://digital.copcomm.com/i/14320/19)).

**Key shots first, then the sequence follows.** A lead lighter lights a few key shots from the art department's keys and builds the sequence's light rigs. The rigs are then propagated to the production shots and handed to other lighters. Sources: DreamWorks ([lead lighter listing](https://www.nbcunicareers.com/node/96643)), Laika ([listing](https://zerply.com/industry-jobs/Lead-CG-Lighter-Laika-Hillsboro-29Fe4r6u)), and the propagation step ([PI Studios](https://pistudios.com/?p=1587)). Sony Pictures Animation's *Hotel Transylvania 3* set-up puts one sequence "key gaffer" over several key-shot branches, so a change at the top updates every shot ([SIGGRAPH 2018 talk](https://history.siggraph.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/2018-Talks-Chen_GafFour-and-Sequence-based-Lighting.pdf)). These descriptions come from job postings and one talk, but they agree.

**Hero shots.** Key or hero shots are flagged in tracking and set the standard for the rest of a sequence. Netflix's vendor tracking has a field to mark them ([Netflix Partner Help](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/360062320974-Status-Reporting-Instructions); a VFX source, but the practice is shared).

## 3. Review and approval cadence

**Dailies.** Shots are shown to supervisors and the director as they progress. A Pixar animator describes animation dailies most mornings in a large screening room, plus smaller, less formal "walk-thrus" with the director ([Animation Mentor interview](https://www.animationmentor.com/?p=4832), **uncertain**: secondary). Catmull describes dailies as a place to show unfinished work early, with candour encouraged ([CS Monitor review of *Creativity, Inc.*](https://csmonitor.com/Books/Book-Reviews/2014/0414/Creativity-Inc)).

**Director reviews and the "sweatbox".** At Disney, the director's review room was historically called the sweatbox. There the director approves scenes or asks for changes, with producers, production staff and department supervisors present and the animator called in to take notes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweat_box); [Cartoon Brew on *Fantasia* notes](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/classic/john-canemaker-unlocks-the-secrets-of-fantasias-sweatbox-notes-109312.html)).

**Approvals in stages.** Approvals come at the blocking gate (the director's main one), then at polish and final ([Disney, Frozen 2](https://www.disney.co.uk/movies/frozen-2/behind-the-scenes-of-frozen-2)). Status vocabularies such as "final", "buyoff" or "approved" are studio-specific; I found no public standard **(varies)**. A common pattern keeps a few general statuses on the shot and the detailed ones on each department task ([ShotGrid community](https://community.shotgridsoftware.com/t/rules-for-summarized-pipeline-statuses/17819)).

**Whole-film screenings.** Pixar put *Soul* "up in the big theater" every four months for outside departments, collecting notes through a form, and used the screenings as schedule checks ([Nolting](https://www.provideocoalition.com/art-of-the-cut-soul/amp/)). The Braintrust of senior storytellers gives candid notes on these reels but has no authority over the director ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3027135/inside-the-pixar-braintrust)). *Spider-Verse* held five or six temp previews, the early ones made of storyboards ([Fisher](https://www.provideocoalition.com/aotc-spiderverse)).

**Work in context.** Shots are judged in the cut with their neighbours, not alone. Changing one shot's length on *Spider-Verse* often changed the pacing of three or four shots around it ([Fisher](https://www.provideocoalition.com/aotc-spiderverse)). Flow Production Tracking's review tools show history and let reviewers review "in the context of the final product" ([Autodesk](https://www.autodesk.com/products/flow-production-tracking/product-details); [features](https://www.autodesk.com/eu/products/shotgrid/features)).

**Tracking notes.** Production tracking systems hold the versions, playlists for review sessions, notes per version and statuses. Autodesk Flow Production Tracking (formerly ShotGrid) is the common commercial one ([Autodesk features](https://www.autodesk.com/eu/products/shotgrid/features)). Large studios also run in-house tools **(varies)**.

**When upstream assets change after approval.** Studios guard approved shots in three main ways:
- Version pinning: a Pixar patent describes "pinning" asset versions, so an approved shot can be re-rendered exactly, and notes that approved images often need rework later ([USRE44369](https://patents.google.com/patent/USRE44369)).
- Layered scene description: in USD, each department writes its own override layer, so one department's edits are non-destructive to others ([Foundry](https://learn.foundry.com/nuke/content/comp_environment/usd-3d-comp/usd-basics.html)). DreamWorks ranks downstream departments' layers above upstream ones ([DreamWorks, Zero to USD](https://research.dreamworks.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/Zero-to-USD-with-notes.pdf)).
- Locked deliveries: assets are finished before downstream departments get them ([Animal Logic, Atomic Assets](https://animallogic.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/The-Power-of-Atomic-Assests.pdf)), and rerunnable shot setups handle re-deliveries ([Animal Logic](https://animallogic.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Lego-Batman-FX-Objects-Variations-and-Automation-Alternatives-to-Shot-Based-FX.pdf)).

When a shot is updated, some studios regenerate a daily automatically for review ([MPC, *The Jungle Book*](https://mpcvfx.com/en/news/rapid-high-quality-dailies-for-the-jungle-book)). The policy for when an approved shot is reopened is studio-specific **(uncertain)**.

## 4. 2D/hand-drawn and TV-series differences

- **Animatic → timing → service studio.** In US TV, boards and the dialogue recording are cut into an animatic. Timing directors then write exposure sheets (X-sheets) with frame-by-frame instructions, timing and lip shapes, and send them with the animatic to an overseas service studio, which draws the frames ([Cartoon Brew, Directing TV Animation](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/old-brew/directing-tv-animation-part-1-167.html); on *The Simpsons* and AKOM, [academic study](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4q04s8jv)). The X-sheet is a frame-by-frame grid of drawing exposures, planned before the drawings are made ([Toon Boom docs](https://docs.toonboom.com/help/harmony-22/essentials/timing/about-creating-exposure.html)).
- **Distance and lag.** TV directors are rarely in the same place as the animators, and the work takes weeks to come back ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/old-brew/directing-tv-animation-part-1-167.html)).
- **Retakes.** The service studio delivers shot by shot; the director and supervisor issue retake notes. Each retake is either sent back overseas or fixed in-house, and retakes for one episode can take up to six weeks ([CG Wire](https://blog.cg-wire.com/how-to-handle-animation-retakes-in-a-cartoon-tv-show); [Outsourcing of animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outsourcing_of_animation); **uncertain**: practitioner and secondary sources). Retakes cost money, so clients' "creative retakes" are a known budget pressure ([KOCCA report](https://kocca.kr/knowledge/abroad/indu/__icsFiles/afieldfile/2010/05/28/eOCmN8N35xfV.pdf)).
- **Leica reel.** This is the older name for the story reel. It is said to come from the Leica cameras Disney used to film boards ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leica_reel)); the origin is **uncertain** and rests largely on Leica marketing.
- **Cleanup.** Hand-drawn features add rough animation → cleanup → ink and paint ([Wikipedia: Cleanup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleanup_(animation))).

## 5. Why studios work this way

- **Changes get more expensive downstream.** Boards are cheap to redraw, while re-animating, re-rendering or re-recording star actors is not ([discussion](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/6161523-no-amount-of-technology-will-make-a-bad-story-good), **uncertain**). Lee Unkrich: animation is so costly that the team must know "exactly what we need for every single frame" before animating ([Post Magazine](https://digital.copcomm.com/i/13405/14)). Steve Jobs put it as: we "build our movie before we make it" ([AWN/press report](https://www.awn.com/mag/issue4.09/4.09pages/cohentoystory24.php3), **uncertain**: secondary).
- **Gate sequences on a working reel.** Lasseter is quoted that no sequence goes into production until its story reel works ([secondary](https://denki.co.uk/?p=9285), **uncertain**).
- **Locks in order.** A typical order is story/reel approval, then layout (camera and cut lengths), then animation final, then lighting final and picture lock. Later departments build on the earlier decisions, so reopening an early one invalidates the work after it ([picture lock explainer](https://skidmore.substack.com/p/picture-lock)). Studios rarely publish their formal lock names **(varies)**.
- **Iterate the cheapest form.** The reel is rebuilt many times over years, and screened, cut and re-boarded. Early versions are expected to be "ugly babies" that need protecting while they improve ([Catmull summary](https://mindsetonline.com/ed-catmull-pixar-30-years-braintrust-ugly-work-early/)). Pixar can finish one act ahead of the others to debug the production ([Bordwell](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2008/04/10/a-glimpse-into-the-pixar-kitchen/)).
- **Look decided once per sequence.** Colour scripts and lit key shots keep hundreds of shots consistent and let many lighters work in parallel from one approved reference.

## Gaps

- No first-party, public definition found for "shot package", "buyoff" or "finals" status names; these are studio-internal.
- Daily-dailies cadence at Pixar rests on a secondary interview (fetch blocked); Braintrust meeting frequency was not confirmed beyond Nolting's four-month screenings.
- The VES Handbook, Richard Williams' *Animator's Survival Kit* and *The Art of Pixar* were not accessible online; claims are not drawn from them.
- The SIGGRAPH 2018 sequence-lighting PDF could not be text-extracted; its summary comes from the search index.
- No Animation Guild material on pipeline stages was found.
