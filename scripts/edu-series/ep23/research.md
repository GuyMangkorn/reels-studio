## What the 1998 study measured

- **Experiment 1 (SRC001, pp. 645–646).** The paper reports 15 pedestrians on Cornell’s campus, approximately 20–65 years old. Researchers approached pedestrians alone or accompanied by one other person (two cases). An experimenter with a campus map asked for directions. A door carried between the pair blocked the pedestrian’s view for about one second while another experimenter took his place and continued asking for directions with an identical map. The experimenters differed in clothing, appearance and voice.
- **Question and classification.** After the directions, the replacement disclosed the study and asked, “Did you notice anything unusual at all when that door passed by a minute ago?” If the pedestrian omitted the switch, the experimenter explicitly asked whether they noticed the person now addressing them was different from the one who approached them for directions. The first answer yielded 7/15 reports of noticing the switch and 8/15 who gave no such report. One pedestrian who initially reported nothing unusual later claimed to have noticed in response to the direct question. Continuing to give directions is a poor proxy for noticing because some who noticed also continued.
- **Experiment 2 (SRC001, pp. 647–648).** Same location and swap procedure, with the same two experimenters dressed as construction workers. All 12 pedestrians were Cornell undergraduate or graduate students. The notice questions were the same, with added disclosure after directions that the apparent construction workers were doing a psychology study. Four of 12 reported the switch on the open-ended question. Five expressed surprise at the reveal, and three others claimed to notice only after prompting but could not accurately describe the differences. The authors treated those three as ambiguous prompted reports. Keep the denominators separate because the sample and costume conditions changed.
- **Interpretation limits.** The observed counts are well documented for these small campus samples and the interview protocol. Silent awareness is hard to rule out from a report alone. The authors discuss the map, the unusual interruption and the possibility of effects from social category or expectations. The study did not precisely measure eye contact at the swap or establish why any individual missed it.
