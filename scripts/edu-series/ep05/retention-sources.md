## Q1. YouTube definitions and recommendations

- **Viewed vs swiped away.** "How many chose to view: Highlights the percentage of times that viewers viewed your Shorts versus swiped away", shown beside "Shown in feed" (SRC02). Another page: "The percentage of times that viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds of a Short" (SRC01). **No timing threshold is published.** SRC08 (secondary report of SRC07) says YouTube keeps exact thresholds private to prevent gaming. Agency figures ("2 s", "~30 s") are guesses (SRC42). *High.*
- **APV.** "Average percentage viewed: … among those who stayed to watch. This is calculated from engaged views and their corresponding watch time." Engaged views are counted "not including any loops" (SRC01). So swipers are excluded from APV. YouTube doesn't explain APV over 100 %; my inference is that loop watch time grows while the loop-free view count doesn't. *Medium.*
- **Public views.** Counted "the moment a video starts to play across all formats, including Shorts" (SRC03). "An engaged view is defined as clicking to watch or watching past the initial seconds" (SRC04). The 1.3k / 2.5k / 24k counts therefore include swiped plays and replays; compare engaged views instead. *High.*
- **Recommendations.** The Shorts Feed "is personalized to reflect what we think you want to see next". Signals include watch history, likes, dislikes, "Not interested" and surveys, which help "understand satisfaction, not just watch time" (SRC05, SRC06). Sherman: the system will "find a seed audience" and then expands or tapers (SRC07). In SRC09 the viewed-vs-swiped-away metric is called "a great one" (speaker attribution from excerpt only), and Sherman says recommendations try to "normalise for duration a bit". The 2026 view change left recommendations unchanged (SRC04). **YouTube has never said swipe rate is a ranking input or how much it weighs.** "70 % viewed = viral" style benchmarks are secondary only (SRC42). *High for what is stated; low for any weights.*

## Q2. First-seconds psychology

- **Curiosity / information gap.** A posed question or puzzle makes a knowledge gap salient and triggers curiosity (SRC12). People spent tokens or waiting time to see answers they were curious about, and later recalled surprising answers better (SRC13). Curiosity also improves memory for incidental material (SRC14). It tracks how close people *feel* to the answer (SRC15). In preregistered studies, making moderate gaps salient increased information seeking (SRC16). *High in the lab, medium for Shorts.* Kang's waiting task is the closest lab version of not swiping.
- **Surprise / expectancy violation.** Confident errors produce surprise and curiosity, which predicted further exploration (SRC17). Infants explored expectation-violating objects more (SRC18). In free-zapping web ads, "Surprise and joy effectively concentrate attention and retain viewers" (SRC19; lab sample, ads). *Medium-high for the mechanism, medium for transfer.*
- **Arousal / threat.** High-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) predicted sharing; so did surprising, interesting and useful content (SRC20). Sharing is not watching. In 12,448 headline RCTs, each negative word raised CTR by 2.3 %, but **fear words lowered it** and effects were small (SRC21). Survival framing helps memory, not attention (SRC29). *Medium; threat alone is unreliable.*
- **"You" framing.** Self-reference aids memory (SRC23); it can help persuasion when arguments are strong but can also reverse (SRC24). Second-person pronouns did **not** reliably predict headline success (β = 0.05, H7 unsupported; SRC22). All three hooks use คุณ, so "you" can't explain EP05. *Medium-high.*
- **Imagery / "what if".** Imagining yourself in a scenario raised likelihood estimates and compliance (SRC25). Imagery appeals backfire when the scene is hard to picture (SRC26). *Low-medium transfer.*
- **Narrative transportation** strengthens story-consistent beliefs (SRC27, SRC28). It measures persuasion, not retention. *Low for 60 s explainers.*

## Q3. Explanation difficulty

Jargon impairs processing and increases resistance (SRC30). It lowers interest and perceived understanding even when defined (SRC31). Needless complexity lowers judgements through fluency (SRC32), a cue that acts across many judgements (SRC33). Headline complexity slightly cut CTR (SRC21). Concreteness best predicted comprehensibility, interest and recall (SRC36). Interesting-but-irrelevant details hurt recall most when placed **at the start** (SRC34; SRC35 not verified). *High for comprehension, medium for Shorts drop-off.*

## Q4. Short-form data (mostly weak)

- **Meta, ads, 2016:** feed content recalled after 0.25 s; 1.7 s average per item on mobile; "up to 47 % of the value" of a video campaign in the first 3 s (SRC39). *Low-medium.*
- **Google ABCD, ads:** "Aim for two or more shots in the first five seconds"; "Faces attract immediate attention"; imagery that makes viewers think or feel in the first 5 s "links to longer view times" (SRC38). Vendor self-report. *Low-medium.*
- **Pacing:** fast pace plus arousing content overloads viewers and lowers recall (SRC37). *Medium.*
- **Voice:** a human voice beat 2003-era TTS on transfer (SRC41); modern TTS untested. *Low.*
- **Hook wording:** curiosity gaps beat direct address in TikTok hooks (SRC40; preprint, engagement not retention). *Low.* "One second to hook" (SRC10) is anecdote.

## Q5. Confounders

1. **n = 3, many features change at once.** No causal attribution is possible.
2. **Different audiences.** Swipe rate is measured on whoever the seed process chose (SRC07); wider reach probably raises swipes (inference). EP05 holding 20 % at 24k is notable but not a like-for-like comparison.
3. **Sampling noise.** If impressions ≈ public views, the standard error is ~1.3 pp at 1.3k and ~0.3 pp at 24k. The gap is real, just uncontrolled.
4. **Topic demand.** Check search terms under Traffic sources (SRC02).
5. **Posting time.** SRC07 has a chapter on it; the answer (matters little except for news) was seen only in SRC42 reports. *Low confidence.*
6. **Momentum and timing.** Channel history; the 1 Oct 2026 originality change (SRC11, lead-only).
7. **Cover and title** barely matter in an autoplay feed (SRC10, creator claim) but can shift the traffic mix.
8. **Geography, loops, length.** Check Audience geography. EP05's 91 % may include loops. The 60–66 s length gap is minor.

## Implications for EP05 vs EP02/EP03

**Well supported.**
- Swipe rate measures the opening; APV measures only those who stayed. No threshold is published.
- A salient, near-answerable question plus a violated expectation increases waiting and information seeking (SRC13, SRC15–SRC17).
- Jargon and abstraction reduce fluency and interest (SRC30, SRC31, SRC36).

**Plausible, untested in Shorts.** EP05's hook stacks:
- an easy-to-picture scene with the viewer in it (SRC25, SRC26);
- a counterintuitive, high-stakes claim that raises arousal (SRC19, SRC20);
- an early answer to the obvious objection, which may block disbelief and keep the gap answerable (SRC15; inference).

EP02 and EP03 are also counterintuitive, so surprise alone doesn't explain the gap.

**Speculation.**
- EP03 assumes the viewer owns a dog, which may break self-reference for everyone else.
- EP05's 91 % depends on the body of the video (payoff timing, loop seam), which wasn't reviewed.
- Topic demand and audience match may explain much of the gap.
