# EDU-EP04-SHORT — research packet (W1.1, Boat)

- Deliverable: `EDU-EP04-SHORT` (Short, Thai, educational psychology, concept C4, `concepts.md`). Research date: 2026-11-05 (all access dates below).
- Mode: educational explainer on a real, everyday phenomenon. Central question: why does the next queue seem faster, and why do switches seem to backfire? Takeaway: it is partly math, partly attention and memory, and a shared single queue removes the choice.
- Tooling limits: web search and page fetch only, through summarizing tools; no page read as raw text. Paywalled journal pages were not opened. Abstracts came from search/aggregator excerpts, so every wording is second-hand and the status is `lead-only` unless stated.

| F001 | Beat 1: People often switch queues; it frequently does not help. A switch from a queue to an equally fast alternative gains nothing in expectation, and switchers can end up waiting longer in studies. | documented (partly) | SRC002 (switching rises when you are last); SRC003 (switchers waited longer, secondary, numbers unverified). Whether switching hurts in general depends on real information about the lines. Observational and online-lab designs. | Moderate for "switching is common and often not a gain". Low for any percentage. |
| F002 | Beat 2 (model result): With three queues, if every queue is identical and independent, your queue is the fastest of the three with probability 1/3, so some other queue beats yours with probability 2/3. | well-supported theory (symmetry argument, elementary) | Assumptions: identical servers and customer mix, independent random service times, no ties, "fastest" means the earliest finishing the same number of customers or reaching the till first, and you judge after the fact. Reality differs: baskets size, price checks, cashier speed and the queue lengths you see. Also: it is the chance your line wins, not that you are "always unlucky". "At least one other line beats yours" is 2/3, not "the other line is always faster". Qminder SRC009 gives 25% for four lines; same logic, marketing blog. No peer-reviewed source found stating the 1-in-3 number; it is a symmetry calculation (n identical lines give 1/n). | High for the math under the stated assumptions. Label as "model" on screen. |
| F003 | Beat 2 follow-up: If you move to another line mid-wait, the line you join is not better in expectation than the one you left, if the lines are alike and you do not know more about them. | well-supported theory | Same symmetry/memoryless assumptions. Real queues carry information (shorter line, fewer big baskets). Real tills break the symmetry. SRC002 supports the empirical side only indirectly. | Moderate |
| F004 | Beat 3: Overtakes both ways happen equally often in a queueing system: the total number of "slips" (people who are overtaken, in Larson's terms) equals the total number of "skips" (people who overtake). | documented (theory result) | SRC001 excerpts (via search). Defined for FIFO violation across a whole system; I did not read the derivation. Supports the symmetry idea "you are overtaken and you overtake others equally often overall", not the claim that you notice the first more. | Moderate (excerpt-based) |
| F005 | Beat 3: People notice the lines that move past them more than the ones they move past, because attention is on the other lines while their own line is not "news" and being passed feels like an injustice. | speculation / unverified as a queue-specific finding | Larson (SRC001) argues FIFO violations anger people (injustice); Don Norman's version of the asymmetry appears only in SRC009, not traced to a primary text. No queue experiment of this exact claim found. What would settle it: an experiment recording perceived vs actual overtakes (e.g. in Buell/Rafaeli datasets) or a primary Norman text. Fair wording: "tends to", "psychologists suggest", do not claim a measured effect. | Low-moderate. Mechanism is plausible, not demonstrated. |
| F006 | Beat 3 (supporting): Last place feels worse; people switch more when nobody is behind them. | documented (lab and one grocery observation) | SRC002 abstract fragments. Switching rises when last. Context: shows how feelings of position drive switches. | Moderate |
| F007 | Beat 4: Bad events tend to have a stronger psychological impact than good events of the same size, and bad events are remembered and processed more thoroughly. | well-supported theory (large review) | SRC006 abstract: "bad is stronger than good" across many domains. Review is broad, with some conditions where good is stronger. Not queue-specific. | Moderate-high as a general principle |
| F008 | Beat 4: People judge an episode by its worst moment and its ending, with duration counting little (peak-end, duration neglect). | documented in lab (pain, other episodes); not tested for queues | SRC007, search summaries only. A waiting episode that ends badly may be judged worse than its length suggests. Not shown for the "I always get the slow line" belief. | Moderate for the rule; low for application to queue memory |
| F009 | Beat 4 link: "I'm always unlucky" is partly an effect of remembering the bad runs. | speculation | Combines F005/F007/F008; no direct test found (illusory correlation between joining a line and it slowing is repeated in SRC009 without a primary source).
