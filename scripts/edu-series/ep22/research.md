## 1. Short answer (what is attested)

1. Tasting before the ruler eats is a real, documented court rule in several Chinese dynasties. The taster was not always a eunuch.
   - Tang: `尚食奉御` (a palace officer) must taste first. Source: Tang Liu Dian vol. 11.
   - Ming: the written rule gives the duty to a woman official, `尚食` (the head of the Bureau of Food among the six women's bureaus), not to a eunuch. Source: Ming Shi, Zhi 50 (Zhi Guan San), vol. 74. The eunuch office `尚膳监` ran the kitchen and banquets; the text gives it no tasting line.
   - Qing (Qianlong and later): attendant eunuchs `尝膳`, tasting a little of each dish after the table was set. Sources: Palace Museum site (Qianlong-era procedure, as its text is not tied to a reign, see claim C07) and Puyi's memoir (Xuantong years, 1909–1912).
2. Silver was a separate check and it was weak. Silver darkens on sulfur, not on pure arsenic; the old arsenic worked on silver because it was impure.
3. The best surprising and true detail is in Puyi's own memoir: the Qing court ran the whole silver-tag and eunuch-tasting ritual on a table of about 28 dishes that he never ate. He ate dishes sent by the empress dowager and the consorts. The memoir calls the imperial-kitchen table show.
4. There is one concrete, dated case where eunuchs were made to taste: the Korean 1898 "coffee plot" (Gojong, Seoul). Korea Times (historian Robert Neff): Gojong ordered two eunuchs to drink the coffee; the senior one drank half a cup and fell ill. It is a Korean court, not a Chinese one, and the details differ between accounts.
5. No named Chinese eunuch food taster with a documented name or case was found. Popular articles name one ("Li Wei", Wanli); that name could not be verified and must not be used.
6. The 1898 plot and Guangxu (arsenic, 2008 report) both show that the ritual did not stop poisoning that came by another route. For Guangxu the report names no poisoner.

## 4. Separation: documented, legend, drama, internet myth

- Documented rule: Tang 尚食奉御 tasting (S03); Ming Shi's 尚食 tasting rule (S04); Qing eunuch tasting (S01, S02).
- Documented but first-person: Puyi's account of silver tags, tasting and the display table (S01).
- Documented case with caveats: Korea 1898 coffee plot (S08, with S09/S10 disagreeing on who drank).
- Legend or internet myth: "Li Wei, Wanli taster" (S16); "silver detects any poison" (S06 contradicts); "three-spoon rule" (no source found).
- Drama and fiction: The Apothecary Diaries and Chinese palace series show a poison-taster heroine or eunuch; none of it is evidence (S17). Wording in the script must not use drama scenes as history.

## 5. Hook candidates

### H1 (recommended): "The eunuch tasted a table the emperor never ate"
- Hook idea (ER01): you believe a eunuch tasting is what kept the emperor from poison; in the last Qing court the memoir says the emperor never touched the imperial-kitchen table (almost 30 dishes) and ate what the empress dowager sent.
- Rests on: C03, C04, C05; C06 only as implied, never stated. Source: S01 (first-person), S02.
- Stakes a picture can show: a long table of silver-lidded dishes; a small eunuch calling "打碗盖" and lifting the lids; the child emperor eating something else from a smaller side tray.
- Strength: true, surprising, concrete, visual, verifiable at primary-memoir level, with the silver-tag detail as a second beat.
- Risks: the narrator must not say "the eunuch only tasted dishes nobody ate" (C06). Puyi is a memoir written in custody; the narration must frame it as his own recollection. The tasters here are Qing; the ขันที role is true for this court.

### H2: "The tasting job in the Ming was not for eunuchs" (the correction)
- Hook idea: you picture a eunuch tasting the emperor's food; in the Ming Shi the tasting rule names a woman official, and the eunuchs ran the kitchen.
- Rests on: C02. Sources S04 (primary). Practice after the early Ming is unproven (S05 weak).
- Strength: contradicts the viewer's certainty directly and is a clean ER01 hook.
- Risks: it fights the user's chosen "ขันทีชิมยาพิษ" premise and is hard to show in a picture; the viewer's belief is true for the Qing. Good as a second beat, weak as the lead.

### H3: "Silver did not catch the poison; it caught the dirt in it"
- Hook idea: the famous silver needle test never reacted to arsenic itself; it reacted to the sulfur mixed in.
- Rests on: C07, C08. Sources S06 (popular), S07 (lead). Chemistry is standard but the packet's sourcing is popular-science level.
- Strength: visual (silver turning black, a boiled egg yolk blackening a spoon), explains the real limit, fits a "one rule that flips" structure (ER03).
- Risks: weak link to the eunuch; a school-science chain is one more mechanism step (ER09 limit about 10 s); needs a sturdier chemistry source for a safe grade.

### H4 (not recommended yet): Korea 1898, "the emperor made two eunuchs drink first"
- Rests on: C10, a single source with conflicting accounts; Korean not Chinese; ขันที is true here. Strongest story beat (a concrete named date and a coffee) but only usable after a second source for the eunuch detail.

Lead with H1 (Puyi table) as the hook and payoff; use H3's one clean line ("silver darkens on sulfur, not on poison") as the second problem, and the Tang or Ming rule as an optional "who tasted" beat only if it fits ER09 timing. Do not use H4 or Guangxu until corroborated. A possible one-rule structure (for Preaw to decide, not a script): "the ritual protected the show, not the meal" would overstate C06; a safer rule is "the safety ritual was real, but checks were many and each was weak."

Possible chain, for orientation only: belief (eunuch tastes, silver warns) → the Qing ritual in Puyi's own words → almost 30 dishes he never ate → silver's real limit. Hold at under 10 s each (ER09).
